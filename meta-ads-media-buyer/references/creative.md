## 1. Why creative decides performance

With broad targeting, the ad selects its own audience: the people who stop, watch and click teach the system who to show it to next. Meta's ad retrieval also groups near-identical ads together, so ten small variations of one idea behave like one ad. Real diversity (different angles, formats, people, visuals) is what opens new audiences and keeps costs stable at scale.

## 2. From research to angles

Good ads come from customer language, not from brainstorming in a vacuum. Ask the user for, or look for:
- Reviews, testimonials, support tickets, sales call notes, comments on existing ads
- Competitor reviews (what people love and resent)
- Competitor ads in the Meta Ad Library: which concepts have been running longest

Then build angles. An angle is a reason this person should care now. Useful families:

| Angle family | Example framing |
|---|---|
| Problem and solution | Name the frustration precisely, then show the fix |
| Outcome or transformation | Life after the product; before and after where policy allows |
| Social proof | Customer story, review montage, numbers served, expert endorsement |
| Mechanism | Why it works when other things did not |
| Comparison | Against the old way or the generic alternative (not naming rivals unfairly) |
| Objection handling | Price, trust, effort, "will it work for me" |
| Offer and urgency | Bundle, guarantee, limited stock or deadline that is real |
| Identity | "For people who..." speaking to a specific group's situation without asserting personal attributes |
| Education | Teach something useful, product as the natural next step |
| Founder or behind the scenes | Who makes it and why |

Match the message to awareness: people who do not know the problem need the problem dramatised; people comparing options need proof and differentiation; warm audiences need a reason to act today.

## 3. Formats

- **UGC-style video** (creator or customer speaking to camera): usually the strongest prospecting format for consumer products.
- **Founder or expert video**: trust for services, B2B, high-ticket.
- **Demo or how-it-works video**: products whose value is visible.
- **Static image**: fast to produce and test; strong for offers, comparisons, review screenshots, bold claims with proof. Often wins on cost for retargeting.
- **Carousel**: multiple products, steps, features or a story across cards.
- **Catalog and dynamic product ads**: many SKUs, retargeting viewed products.
- **Instant form or messaging ads**: when conversion happens off-site.
- **Partnership ads** (run from a creator's handle): borrowed credibility; typically lifts click-through.

A healthy account runs a mix. If everything is one format, the first test to propose is a different one.

## 4. Building a video ad

Structure: **Hook (0 to 3 s) → Problem or promise → Proof or demo → Offer → Call to action.**

- The first frame and first line decide whether anything else is seen. Open with motion, a face, the product in use, or a striking claim. Put the hook in on-screen text as well as audio.
- Design for sound off: captions throughout, large enough to read on a phone.
- Show the product early. Keep cuts quick. Most direct-response ads work best at 15 to 45 seconds; longer is fine when it holds attention.
- Shoot or crop vertical 9:16 and keep key elements inside the safe zone.
- End with one clear action.

**Hook patterns to adapt** (write them in the customer's own words):
- Call out the problem: "If your [thing] keeps [problem], watch this."
- Surprising result: "I stopped [common habit] and [specific outcome]."
- Contrarian: "You do not need [expensive alternative] to [goal]."
- Question: "Why does nobody tell you [insight]?"
- Demonstration: start mid-action with the product solving the problem.
- Social proof: "[Number] people switched to this in [period]. Here is why."
- Direct offer: "[Offer], until [real deadline]."

When asked for hooks or scripts, give several distinct options spread across angles rather than rewordings of one.

## 5. Writing copy

- **Primary text**: the first line must earn the "see more" (about 125 characters show before truncation). Lead with the benefit or the hook, then proof, then the offer and call to action. Test a short version against a longer story version.
- **Headline**: the offer or main benefit in a few words (roughly 40 characters or fewer displays cleanly).
- **Description**: optional supporting detail such as shipping, guarantee or rating.
- **CTA button**: match the intent. "Shop now" for ready-to-buy offers, "Learn more" for considered purchases, "Send message" or "Sign up" as appropriate.
- Write the way the customer speaks, in their language and dialect. Be specific: numbers, names, timeframes. Cut vague superlatives.
- Keep the ad and landing page consistent: same promise, same price, same visual.
- Stay inside policy: no claims you cannot substantiate, no implying knowledge of personal attributes ("Are you diabetic?"), no unrealistic outcomes. See `policy-and-compliance.md`.

Supplying several text and headline variations per ad lets Meta mix them, but it hides which line won. Use it for scaling; use fixed combinations when the point is to learn.

## 6. Testing method

1. **Test concepts before details.** Order of impact: angle and offer, then format, then hook, then body and copy, then small edits.
2. **Structure.** A dedicated testing campaign or ad set with broad targeting, the same conversion event as the scaling campaign, and three to six creatives per batch. Too many ads in one ad set starves most of them of spend.
3. **Budget.** Enough for each concept to reach about 1 to 3 times target CPA in spend. With very small budgets, test fewer things and accept slower learning.
4. **Uneven spend is information.** Meta pushes budget to the ads it predicts will win. An ad that receives almost no spend usually has weak early signals. To force a fair read, use ad set budgets with one concept per ad set, or Meta's A/B test tool.
5. **Judge on the real goal.** CPA or ROAS decides. CTR and hook rate explain why, and guide the next iteration.
6. **Graduate winners.** Move them into the scaling campaign using the existing post ID so social proof carries over.
7. **Iterate on winners.** New hooks on a winning body, the same script with a different creator, the winning static turned into a video, the winning angle in a new format.
8. **Keep a log.** Concept, angle, format, hook, date, spend, result, lesson. See `templates.md`.

Cadence: fresh creative every one to two weeks at meaningful spend. Higher budgets burn through creative faster.

## 7. Reading creative metrics

| Metric | Formula | What it tells you |
|---|---|---|
| Hook rate (thumb-stop) | 3-second video plays / impressions | Strength of the opening. Around 25 to 30 percent or more is healthy for video; compare with the account's own norm |
| Hold rate | ThruPlays / 3-second plays | Whether the middle keeps people |
| Link CTR | Link clicks / impressions | Whether the message makes people want more. About 1 percent is a common reference; varies widely |
| Cost per landing page view | Spend / landing page views | Combined cost of attention and click |
| Conversion rate | Purchases or leads / landing page views | Offer and page fit with the click |

Combinations:
- High hook, low CTR: interesting opening, weak pitch or call to action. Rework the body and offer.
- Low hook, good CTR among viewers: good message hidden behind a weak opening. Replace the first three seconds.
- High CTR, low conversion: the ad attracts curiosity rather than buyers, or the page breaks the promise.
- Good CPA at low spend that worsens with budget: narrow appeal. Needs broader angles.

## 8. Fatigue and refresh

Signs: frequency climbing while CTR falls and CPM or CPA rises on a creative that used to perform, over one to two weeks rather than one bad day. Response: introduce new concepts rather than nudging the old one, keep the fatigued winner running at lower spend while replacements prove themselves, and bring it back later (rested ads often work again).

## 9. Specs

Confirm current limits in Meta's ads guide; these are the working defaults.

| Placement | Ratio | Recommended size |
|---|---|---|
| Feed image or video | 4:5 (or 1:1) | 1080 x 1350 (or 1080 x 1080) |
| Stories and Reels | 9:16 | 1080 x 1920 |
| Carousel | 1:1 or 4:5 | 1080 x 1080 or 1080 x 1350, 2 to 10 cards |
| Right column, some link placements | 1.91:1 | 1200 x 628 |

- Safe zones for 9:16: keep text, logos and faces out of roughly the top 14 percent and the bottom 35 percent on Reels (about the bottom 20 percent on Stories), where interface elements sit.
- Video: MP4 or MOV, H.264, captions burned in or uploaded.
- There is no longer a 20 percent text rule, but cluttered images still tend to perform worse.
